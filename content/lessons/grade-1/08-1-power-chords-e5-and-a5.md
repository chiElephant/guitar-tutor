A power chord is two notes, played on two strings, and it is the sound of rock guitar. There is no major or minor in it, which is why it fits so many songs. E5 and A5 are the open-position versions and they need only one finger.

```chords
E5 A5 D5
```

## E5

Finger 1 on string 5 (A) at fret 2. String 6 open. Play **only** strings 6 and 5. Everything else stays silent.

## A5

Same idea, one string over: finger 1 on string 4 (D) at fret 2, string 5 open. Play only strings 5 and 4.

## D5

And once more: finger 1 on string 3 (G) at fret 2, string 4 open. Strings 4 and 3 only.

## Keeping the other strings quiet

The challenge is aiming. Rest the side of your strumming hand lightly on the strings you are not playing, near the bridge, and let the pick hit only the pair you want. Downstrokes only. Short, firm, even. On an electric guitar with a bit of overdrive this sounds enormous; on an acoustic it sounds punchy and percussive, which is just as useful.

```alphatex

```

Eight downstrokes per bar: E5 for two beats, A5 for two, D5 for two, back to E5. The chord changes are just your first finger stepping across one string.

## Why it matters

Beyond the sound, power chords teach two things you will need in Grade 2 and 3: hitting a specific group of strings on purpose, and keeping a steady stream of eighth notes with your picking hand. Both skills transfer straight to palm muting and barre chords.

## Practice

- E5 only, eight downstrokes per bar, 60 bpm, one minute. Listen for stray strings.
- E5 | A5 | D5 | A5, two beats each, one minute.
- Same progression, but strum lightly on beats 1 and 3 and harder on 2 and 4.
